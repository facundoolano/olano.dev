** Writing with (out) LLMs

I occasionally get a piece on my feed about why using LLMs for writing is not such a good idea. The first one that caught my attention was an Oxide's [[https://rfd.shared.oxide.computer/rfd/0576][document on LLM usage]] that circulated last year. They focus on the "social contract" underlying written communication, and how signs of LLMs usage will undermine the reader's trust:

#+begin_quote
If prose is LLM-generated this social contract becomes ripped up: a reader cannot assume that the writer understands their ideas because they might not so much have read the product of the LLM that they tasked to write it. If one is lucky, these are LLM hallucinations: obviously wrong and quickly discarded. If one is unlucky, however, it will be a kind of LLM-induced cognitive dissonance: a puzzle in which pieces don’t fit because there is in fact no puzzle at all. This can leave a reader frustrated: why should they spend more time reading prose than the writer spent writing it?
#+end_quote

Some could argue that the problem there is not so much LLM involvement but the author's sloppiness: if they did the thinking and reviewed the output then there would be no risk of losing the reader's trust. [[https://eschwitz.substack.com/p/ai-slop-and-evidence-about-evidence][Eric Schwitzgebel argues that]], even when used "responsibly", reviewing and verifying the output, something is lost in translation:

#+begin_quote
There’s a huge cognitive difference between nodding along while reading something and /actually productively generating a text/. Two reasons: First, once the text is on the page, it’s easy to passively let the approximate word suffice, rather than thinking about word choice in the same effortful, active way we do when generating prose de novo. Second, I doubt that human beings, even experts, have a good sense of all the factors that shape word choice -- everything they’re being sensitive to. You would have phrased it slightly differently, and even if you don’t know that, or why, a different signal is sent and received.
#+end_quote

Even if there are no hallucinations and "the puzzle pieces fit", confidence is lost:

#+begin_quote
If I know that an email or an article was written by a respected colleague, reflecting their positive creative exertion in trying to choose the right words, guided by their intuitive expertise in how to phrase things, I have better reason to take it seriously than if I know that it was generated by an LLM and reflects only their passive after-the-fact assent.
#+end_quote

From a systems perspective, [[https://muratbuffalo.blogspot.com/2026/08/the-safest-job-from-ai-may-be-writing.html][Murat Demirbas argues]] that, despite them being text generation machines, LLMs aren't getting better at writing and probably won't:

#+begin_quote
Writing is the ultimate wicked problem, because (...) there is no well defined specification to the task at hand, and there is no ground truth or objective verification of the output. The ultimate measure of success is resonance inside another human mind. (...) Since LLMs lack an active mental model of a specific human reader, they are just optimizing for the statistical probability of the next word over a vast dataset. They cannot empathize with the human reader, as they don't have the human lived experience. And they have zero skin in the game.
#+end_quote

Although these look at the issue from different angles, there's a common thread: writing is not a content-producing problem but a form of communication between writer and reader, a dialogue. Putting an LLM in the middle just disrupts that exchange. I can't resist linking this to how Borges, in his [[http://www.autodidactproject.org/other/borges42_shaw06.html][Note on Bernard Shaw]], dismissed combinatorial literature:

#+begin_quote
Those who practice this game forget that a book is more than a verbal structure or series of verbal structures; it is the dialogue it establishes with its reader and the intonation it imposes upon his voice and the changing and durable images it leaves in his memory. (...) A book is not an isolated being: it is a relationship, an axis of innumerable relationships. One literature differs from another, prior or posterior, less because of the text than because of the way in which it is read: if I were granted the possibility of reading any present-day page—this one, for example—as it will be read in the year two thousand, I would know what the literature of the year two thousand will be like. The conception of literature as a formalistic game leads, in the best of cases, to the fine chiseling of a period or a stanza, to an artful decorum, and in the worst, to the discomforts of a work made of surprises dictated by vanity and chance.
#+end_quote

** Code for humans

#+begin_quote
Code relies on formal logic and test-driven feedback. An LLM can generate functional code because compilers, unit tests, and model checkers can instantly catch errors. Verification is largely mechanical.  Unlike coding, which is a single-mind interaction with a deterministic compiler, prose (...) requires the ability to continuously simulate a reader's internal mental state in real time. To write simply and persuasively, you must track what the reader already knows, manage their cognitive load sentence by sentence, and predict how an argument will land.
#+end_quote

#+begin_quote
LLMs are amazingly good at writing code — so much so that there is borderline mass hysteria about LLMs entirely eliminating software engineering as a craft. As with using an LLM to write prose, there is obvious peril here! Unlike prose, however (which really should be handed in a polished form to an LLM to maximize the LLM’s efficacy), LLMs can be quite effective writing code de novo.
#+end_quote


Abelson & Sussman:
#+begin_quote
Programs must be written for people to read, and only incidentally for machines to execute.
#+end_quote

Donald Knuth:
#+begin_quote
Instead of imagining that our main task is to instruct a /computer/ what to do, let us concentrate rather on explaining to /human beings/ what we want a computer to do.
#+end_quote

<This approach to programming sometimes comes across as pretentious, an attempt to elevate a mundane task to something more sophisticated, art-like. But I have found that even in very worse-is-better, get-the-job-done enterprise settings, [[do-programmers-dream-of-electronic-poems][keeping readers in mind]], making the code self-explanatory---from docstrings and comments to module layout and directory structure---is the best tool programmers have for explaining the system---and, through it, the business---to themselves. Likewise, the software development process, with all of its useful friction, is the best we have for aligning on how we understand and model the domain.

** Code for machines

Up until recently, we didn't strongly need to agree on this, though. I could go about my programming work keeping this writer/reader tension as a fundamental concern, and successfully collaborate with programmers that didn't care for it and instead had a more pass/fail conception of quality. I could make my best effort to communicate through my code, and occasionally spend one or two patience tokens <by asking my colleagues to relocate this helper function or add that module docstring, and more or less be happy with the overall shape of the codebase.

<but this balance obviously breaks as soon as agents enter the picture
<This tension was a latent form of the "great ai split">, and of course it broke as soon as we started letting LLM produced code into our codebase.
https://blog.lmorchard.com/2026/03/11/grief-and-the-ai-split/
https://zanlib.dev/blog/do-you-still-read-the-code/

For one, everyone picked up the pace. Nobody can keep up with the pace and the volume of code changes, and the patience tokens are running low. At the rate we go, focusing on file layout seems like a rude annoyance.
Moreover, the code writing itself is mostly spent reviewing specs and LLM generated code. Who has the cognitive capacity to stay on top of all their coworker's PRs. The only way to steer the style of a project is to set up the patterns that we want LLMs to replicate and to enforce specific rules through metrics and linters. Nuanced, subjective ---humane--- notions like "readability" or simplicity, which cannot be automated are impossible to reach and maintain.
<the same process described above operates at the source code level: the agent replaces the human in making hundreds of micro decisions, some perhaps the author wouldn't have noticed they were making. the signal sent and received is different and with each iteration further apart from it source human intent
<But the problem is, then, communication. Despite any measured quality markers, despite reviewing discipline and where developers fall in the accelerator/viber spectrum, I'm convinced that every time an LLM-assisted PR gets merged, the human presence is further blurred from the codebase.
<And with it any possibility using the system as a means to shape a common understanding of the system and the domain it supports.

<This was perhaps to be expected, a natural move from an artisanal to an industrial discipline. We are moving to a world where text is content, source code is an asset, quality is a github CI status.
LLM generated source code may be consistently worse than that handcrafted by experts, but it's likely better than that handcrafted by the average developer, cheaper, and can be produce at scale.
