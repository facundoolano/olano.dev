** Writing with LLMs

I occasionally get a piece in my feed on why using LLMs for writing is not such a good idea. The first one that caught my attention was an Oxide's [[https://rfd.shared.oxide.computer/rfd/0576][document on LLM usage]] that circulated last year. They focus on the "social contract" underlying written communication, and how signs of LLMs usage will undermine the reader's trust:

#+begin_quote
If prose is LLM-generated this social contract becomes ripped up: a reader cannot assume that the writer understands their ideas because they might not so much have read the product of the LLM that they tasked to write it. If one is lucky, these are LLM hallucinations: obviously wrong and quickly discarded. If one is unlucky, however, it will be a kind of LLM-induced cognitive dissonance: a puzzle in which pieces don’t fit because there is in fact no puzzle at all. This can leave a reader frustrated: why should they spend more time reading prose than the writer spent writing it?
#+end_quote

Some could argue that the problem there is not so much LLM involvement but the author's sloppiness: if they did the thinking and reviewed the output then there would be no risk of losing the reader's trust. [[https://eschwitz.substack.com/p/ai-slop-and-evidence-about-evidence][Eric Schwitzgebel argues that]], even when used "responsibly", reviewing and verifying the output, something is lost in translation:

#+begin_quote
There’s a huge cognitive difference between nodding along while reading something and /actually productively generating a text/. Two reasons: First, once the text is on the page, it’s easy to passively let the approximate word suffice, rather than thinking about word choice in the same effortful, active way we do when generating prose de novo. Second, I doubt that human beings, even experts, have a good sense of all the factors that shape word choice -- everything they’re being sensitive to. You would have phrased it slightly differently, and even if you don’t know that, or why, a different signal is sent and received.
#+end_quote

Even if there are no hallucinations and "the puzzle pieces fit", confidence is lost:

#+begin_quote
If I know that an email or an article was written by a respected colleague, reflecting their positive creative exertion in trying to choose the right words, guided by their intuitive expertise in how to phrase things, I have better reason to take it seriously than if I know that it was generated by an LLM and reflects only their passive after-the-fact assent.
#+end_quote

From a systems perspective, [[https://muratbuffalo.blogspot.com/2026/08/the-safest-job-from-ai-may-be-writing.html][Murat Demirbas argues]] that, despite them being text generation machines, LLMs aren't getting better at writing and probably won't:

#+begin_quote
Writing is the ultimate wicked problem, because (...) there is no well defined specification to the task at hand, and there is no ground truth or objective verification of the output. The ultimate measure of success is resonance inside another human mind. (...) Since LLMs lack an active mental model of a specific human reader, they are just optimizing for the statistical probability of the next word over a vast dataset. They cannot empathize with the human reader, as they don't have the human lived experience. And they have zero skin in the game.
#+end_quote

Although these look at the issue from different angles, there's a common thread: writing is not a content-producing problem but a form of communication between writer and reader, a dialogue. Putting an LLM in the middle just disrupts that exchange. I can't resist linking this to how Borges, in his [[http://www.autodidactproject.org/other/borges42_shaw06.html][Note on Bernard Shaw]], dismissed combinatorial literature:

#+begin_quote
Those who practice this game forget that a book is more than a verbal structure or series of verbal structures; it is the dialogue it establishes with its reader and the intonation it imposes upon his voice and the changing and durable images it leaves in his memory. (...) A book is not an isolated being: it is a relationship, an axis of innumerable relationships. One literature differs from another, prior or posterior, less because of the text than because of the way in which it is read: if I were granted the possibility of reading any present-day page—this one, for example—as it will be read in the year two thousand, I would know what the literature of the year two thousand will be like. The conception of literature as a formalistic game leads, in the best of cases, to the fine chiseling of a period or a stanza, to an artful decorum, and in the worst, to the discomforts of a work made of surprises dictated by vanity and chance.
#+end_quote

** Code for humans

I've noticed this pattern, though: those pieces that critique the use of LLMs, when they come from technical people, will contrast prose writing with programming, an activity that is particularly well suited for text generation. From the same Murat's post:

#+begin_quote
Code relies on formal logic and test-driven feedback. An LLM can generate functional code because compilers, unit tests, and model checkers can instantly catch errors. Verification is largely mechanical. Unlike coding, which is a single-mind interaction with a deterministic compiler, prose (...) requires the ability to continuously simulate a reader's internal mental state in real time. To write simply and persuasively, you must track what the reader already knows, manage their cognitive load sentence by sentence, and predict how an argument will land.
#+end_quote

This makes sense, but also makes me very sad inside. It wasn't that long ago that [[code-is-run-more-than-read]["code is read more than written"]] was folk knowledge. Harold Abelson famously wrote that "Programs must be written for people to read, and only incidentally for machines to execute", and Donald Knuth proposed that "Instead of imagining that our main task is to instruct a /computer/ what to do, let us concentrate rather on explaining to /human beings/ what we want a computer to do". In this view, every one of the issues quoted above translates directly to programming: a social contract is broken when generating code takes less than reviewing it; there's cognitive dissonance when code works but it's not quite clear why or how; there's something lost when the naming, commenting, and modularization were passively accepted instead of guided by intuitive expertise---something off when no mental model of another human is informing those decisions.

The code-for-humans assumption may sound idealistic, or too academic, even pretentious---an attempt to elevate a mundane task to something more sophisticated, art-like. It could arguably be obsolete: code was for humans because it needed to be understood in order to be changed, but if we can use LLMs to explain it to us, and to change it for us, does it still matter?

The thing is that by communicating with code we didn't just produce working software: we were also [[software-design-is-knowledge-building][building knowledge]]. Real-world software systems represent, even determine business processes and human interactions. The system is often the best way to understand those processes, a better source-of-truth than what documentation or any one expert can provide. Even in the most worse-is-better, get-the-job-done enterprise environments, code was the best means we programmers had to learn and communicate about the system---and, through it, the business; making it self-explanatory and augmenting it with domain context was the best shot programmers had at collaboratively building a mental model of the problem they were trying to solve.

** Code for machines

Not everyone shared this code-for-humans view. Some programmers, equally invested in doing a good job, would nevertheless prefer to define software quality in terms of objective pass/fail checks: code coverage, linter rules, cyclomatic complexity. In this view, if the system apparently does what it's supposed to do, and automated checks pass, then it must be good software. And if it turns out not to be so good, then there's surely another automation we could introduce to do better next time.

<Up until recently <the code-for-humans and code-for-machines type of programmers> could coexist in a team and collaborate effectively. I could  always take the extra time polishing the human readability of my code and occasionally request for the extra comment or the better modularization when I reviewed other people's work. I would similarly adhere to the automation and structured checks that so contributed with my coworkers' peace of mind. But this balance broke with the introduction of coding agents. For the reasons discussed above, LLMs are terrible at coding for humans (knowledge building and conceptual integrity) but they are very efficient at coding for machines (working code that passes CI). Given the speed and volume of their production, no amount of human effort can counter the effects of LLMs on a codebase. Intentionally or not, teams adopting them turns their projects into code-for-machines only.

We are moving to a world where text is content, source code is an asset, and software quality is a github CI status. LLM generated source code may be consistently worse than that handcrafted by experts, but it's likely better than that handcrafted by the average developer, cheaper, and can be produced at scale.
<The superiority of code-for-humans is founded on folklore and anecdotal evidence; as long as it appears to be more economically viable at producing working software, code-for-machines will prevail.
<This was perhaps to be expected, the typical transition from an artisanal to an industrial form of software production.

** The human communication business

#+begin_quote
The major problems of our work are not so much technological as sociological in nature. The researchers who made fundamental breakthroughs are in a high-tech business. The rest of us are appliers of their work. We use computers and other new technology components to develop our products or to organize our affairs. *Because we go about this work in teams and projects and other tightly knit working groups, we are mostly in the human communication business*. Our successes stem from good human interactions by all participants in the effort, and our failures stem from poor human interactions. The main reason we tend to focus on the technical rather than the human side of the work is not because it's more crucial, but because it's easier to do.
#+end_quote

That's my favorite quote from Peopleware, a book that first came out in 1987. Tech and capital changed enough since then that it would be fair to question if we are still in such a human communication business.

Maybe communication doesn't need to happen at all. Software that's not understood by its maintainers will probably be more fragile and more annoying to use, but not necessarily less economically viable. We can decide that understanding is not a problem anymore and proceed in isolation to build systems as [[https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/7/13/the-tower-keeps-rising/][inescrutable towers of babel]]. Or maybe we can bring back communication, try to place it elsewhere in the process. Code was never an especially good tool for the job, just the best we had in the context of handcrafted software.

If only to recover some of that builder joy, to preserve some of the humanity that risks getting lost, we could step back and ask ourselves: if not in the code, and not in the code generation process, where do people go in this brave new software development lifecycle?
